# Week 2 reading: Strategic Analysis

*A spoken-style walkthrough of the three set readings, written to take about 20 minutes to read aloud (roughly 3,000 words). It is a summary in my own words of Whittington, Regnér, Angwin, Johnson and Scholes (2020), Exploring Strategy, 12th edition, Pearson: Macro-Environment Analysis (pages 36 to 49), Industry and Sector Analysis (pages 64 to 74) and Resources and Capabilities Analysis (pages 94 to 117). For exact wording, use the PDF.*

## Introduction

This week's three readings answer one question: how should a manager understand an organisation's strategic position? The answer has two halves. Look outward, first at the wide macro-environment with PESTEL, then at the industry with Porter's Five Forces. Then look inward, at the organisation's own resources and capabilities, and ask which of them can give it a real edge. SWOT is the tool that joins the two halves together.

## Part one: the macro-environment

The chapter opens with a worry. The macro-environment can seem too big and unpredictable to grasp, so changes creep up on managers until it is too late. Traditional retailers, banks and newspapers were slow to see what the internet would do to them. The chapter offers tools to keep organisations alert.

The main tool is PESTEL: political, economic, social, technological, ecological and legal factors. It is one of several similar frameworks, and its purpose is simply to sort environmental factors into types so that nothing important is missed.

One point is easy to miss. The environment is not only markets. The book separates the market environment, meaning suppliers, customers and competitors, from the nonmarket environment, meaning politicians, regulators, campaign groups, non-governmental organisations and the media. In markets, firms compete for revenue and profit, mainly through price and innovation. In the nonmarket environment they build reputation, influence and legitimacy, through lobbying, public relations and collaboration. Nonmarket factors matter most where governments are powerful, as in defence and healthcare, where consumers are sensitive, as in food, and where political, business and media elites are closely tied together.

Now the six factors. Politics first. The book gives a two-by-two for judging how politicised an industry is. One axis is how directly the state is involved, as customer, supplier, owner or regulator. The other is exposure to civil society, such as campaigners and the media. Defence scores high on both. Food has less state involvement but heavy exposure to campaigners. Canals are often state-owned but face little civil-society pressure. Organisations in politicised environments need political risk analysis, which has two dimensions. Macro versus micro: risk to a whole country, versus risk to a specific firm or sector within it. China may be medium risk overall, but Japanese car firms there face extra danger from nationalist boycotts. And internal versus external: risk from inside a country, versus knock-on effects from abroad, such as an oil price fall hurting Russia and Venezuela while helping India and Japan.

Economics. Watch exchange rates, interest rates and growth, but above all the economic cycle. Growth rises and falls in cycles, and good times do not last. The lesson is about timing. Invest heavily after several boom years and you may be left with expensive over-capacity in the downturn. Become over-cautious after a couple of weak years and you may lack capacity when demand recovers, while rivals who invested take the advantage. So before any big investment, ask where you are in the cycle. Discretionary-spend industries like furniture and cars are especially cyclical, as are high-fixed-cost industries like airlines, where firms cut prices in a downturn to fill capacity and end up in price wars.

Social factors work in two ways. They shape demand and supply, through demographics, the distribution of wealth, geography and culture. And they shape how effective and innovative organisations are, through networks. The book introduces the organisational field: a community of organisations that interact more with each other than with outsiders, including competitors but also regulators, unions and professions. A sociogram maps the connections, and three ideas help you read it. Density: the more links, the better ideas flow, and the more effective the network. Brokers: an organisation connecting two otherwise separate groups tends to be innovative, because it can combine information from both. And central hubs: an organisation connected to many others holds power, because others rely on it. There are also small worlds, where nearly everyone is one or two steps from everyone else. These protect members but are hard for outsiders, such as foreign firms, to enter. The Israeli Unit 8200 illustration shows a tight alumni network feeding a whole cybersecurity industry.

Technology. The aim is to spot where innovation is happening. The book lists five indicators: research and development budgets, patenting, citations, new product announcements and media coverage. Technology roadmaps project future demands and the technologies that might meet them. The graphene example is striking: patent applications rose from under a hundred a year in 2006 to about four thousand a decade later, with China accounting for 58 per cent.

Ecological factors cover pollution, waste and climate change. The book names three challenges: direct pollution obligations, product stewardship across the whole value chain and life cycle, and sustainable development, meaning whether something can be produced indefinitely. The pressure an organisation feels depends on the issue, which weighs more when it is certain, visible and emotive; on the organisational field, since active regulators and dense networks make bad behaviour hard to hide; and on its own leadership and systems. Firms respond for three reasons: responsibility, legitimacy, and competitiveness, because cutting waste cuts costs and green products can earn a premium.

Legal factors cover regulation, tax and competition rules, and deregulation can open opportunities, as it did for low-cost airlines. But the book stretches the legal factor to include informal rules, calling the whole set the institutional environment, the rules of the game. That leads to three varieties of capitalism. Liberal market economies, like the United States and United Kingdom, favour competition, takeovers, funding from financial markets and radical innovation, and welcome foreign firms. Coordinated market economies, like Germany and Japan, favour coordination between firms, bank funding and steady innovation, and are harder for foreign firms to enter. Developmental market economies, like Brazil, China and India, have a strong state role, and foreign firms are often at a disadvantage.

The section ends with a practical warning. PESTEL produces long, messy lists. The remedy is to identify key drivers for change: the factors likely to have the greatest impact on a particular industry. The same trend matters differently across industries. Falling car use might hit supermarkets harder than retail banks. Once you know the key drivers, you can act, for instance a supermarket cutting out-of-town stores and investing in smaller urban sites.

## Part two: industry analysis and the Five Forces

The second reading narrows to the industry. It starts from a simple observation: industries differ in how easily firms in them earn high profits. Pharmaceuticals has done very well historically, while airlines have underperformed. Porter's Five Forces explains why, through five forces: competitive rivalry, the threat of entry, the threat of substitutes, the power of buyers and the power of suppliers. Where these forces are strong, profits are squeezed and the industry is unattractive.

Before assessing any force, define the industry, because a badly drawn boundary spoils everything after it. There are three issues. Do not define it too broadly or too narrowly: for a Stockholm taxi business, the whole Swedish transport industry is too broad, and central Stockholm minicabs too narrow. Respect the value chain: iron ore, steel and car making are separate industries. And consider geography and segment, since European and Chinese airlines differ, as do leisure and freight. Porter's rule of thumb is that they are distinct industries if they differ on more than one force, or differ greatly on one. Note too that the analysis covers the whole industry, not one firm, and it helps to start with rivalry, because that identifies the core players.

Rivalry is driven by five things. Many or evenly matched competitors. Slow industry growth, so any gain comes from a rival. High fixed costs, which push firms to cut prices for volume and trigger price wars. High exit barriers, which keep excess capacity in the market. And low differentiation, as in commodity markets like petrol, where price is the only weapon.

The threat of entry depends on barriers. The book gives five. Scale and experience, including network effects. Access to supply or distribution channels, though e-commerce let Dell and Amazon bypass retailers. Capital requirements, as in pharmaceuticals. Legislation and government action, such as patents and tariffs, which deregulation can remove, as it did in airlines. And expected retaliation: if entrants believe incumbents will fight back hard, that alone can deter them. The banking illustration shows very high barriers that regulators and financial technology start-ups are now trying to weaken.

Substitutes are products that do a similar job but are different in nature, like aluminium for steel. They come from outside the industry and matter even if few customers switch, because the risk of switching caps prices. Eurostar has no direct rival between Paris and London, yet flight prices still limit what it can charge. The measure that matters is the price-to-performance ratio, not price alone.

Buyer power is high when buyers are concentrated, when switching costs are low, when buyers could supply themselves, called backward vertical integration, and when their own profits are low. The book stresses that buyers are the immediate customers, not necessarily consumers. For Procter and Gamble, the buyers are retailers like Tesco, which have far more bargaining power than shoppers. So it introduces the strategic customer, the powerful buyer strategy should be aimed at. In the public sector, that is usually the funder.

Supplier power is the mirror image. It is high when suppliers are concentrated, as in iron ore; when switching costs are high, as with Microsoft's operating system; when suppliers could move forward into the buyer's business, called forward vertical integration; and when their products are differentiated. Suppliers include labour and finance. Star footballers and pilots' unions are examples of suppliers capturing much of the value.

In some industries there is a sixth element: complementors, organisations that make yours more attractive to customers or suppliers. App developers are complementors to Apple. The idea adds cooperation to the picture of competition, sometimes called co-opetition. Related is the idea of network effects: a product becomes more valuable to each user as more people use it, as with eBay and Facebook. Strong network effects can make an industry very attractive, with high barriers and locked-in customers.

What do you do with the analysis? The book says the aim is not to list forces but to judge whether an industry is a good one to compete in, and where a defensible position lies. First, decide which industries to enter or leave. Attractive industries are those where the forces are weak, and one seriously adverse force can spoil an industry. Low entry barriers are usually the wrong reason to enter. Second, manage the forces, because industry structure is not fixed. Raise entry barriers through advertising, buy rivals to reduce rivalry, or differentiate to build loyalty with strong buyers. Third, consider how competitors are affected differently, since rising barriers may squeeze small players while large ones cope.

The reading closes with six steps, worked through with a coffee shop example: define the industry; identify the actors behind each force; work out the underlying factors and overall strength of each force; assess overall attractiveness; assess recent and expected changes; and decide how to position the business.

## Part three: resources and capabilities

The third reading turns inward, starting with a puzzle. Companies in the same industry, in the same environment, perform very differently: BMW has been consistently successful, Saab went out of business. The book's explanation is that the difference lies in the organisations' own resources and capabilities. Two ideas underpin this. Organisations really do differ. And it can be hard to obtain or copy another's resources and capabilities. Together they form the resource-based view of strategy, associated with Jay Barney.

First, definitions. Resources are the assets an organisation has, "what we have", such as machines, cash, people and patents. Capabilities are how those assets are deployed, "what we do". Deployment matters at least as much as ownership, since state-of-the-art equipment is pointless if it is not used well.

Next, threshold versus distinctive. Threshold resources and capabilities are the qualifiers, needed just to compete at all. They keep moving: suppliers to big retailers now need far more sophisticated IT than a decade ago. But threshold resources do not create advantage. Distinctive ones are the winners: valued by customers and hard for rivals to imitate. Apple's brand, technology and design skills are the example. The book also mentions core competences, Hamel and Prahalad's term for bundles of linked skills and technologies, which stay unique because they are bundled.

Then the heart of the chapter, the VRIO test, with four questions.

V is for value. Does it create something customers value, and let the firm seize opportunities or neutralise threats, at a cost that leaves a return? Being different is not enough. IKEA's cost-conscious culture is the example of value done well.

R is for rarity. If a valuable resource is common among rivals, they can respond quickly, and you get only parity. Car makers adding accessories are the example. Rare resources, such as patents, brands or prime locations, last longer, though rarity should never be assumed to last.

I is for inimitability, which gets the most space. Tangible resources can usually be bought or copied eventually, so advantage more often lies in capabilities. The book gives three sources of inimitability. Complexity: tightly knit internal linkages built up over years, and external links with customers and partners. Even with case studies on IKEA and Ryanair widely available, rivals cannot easily replicate this. Causal ambiguity: competitors cannot work out what causes the advantage, either because it rests on tacit knowledge, or because they cannot see which activities depend on which. Sometimes managers themselves do not fully understand it. And culture and history: taken-for-granted routines and path dependency, the particular history through which capabilities developed, which cannot be copied. Tacit knowledge, personal and hard to communicate, is harder to imitate than explicit knowledge that can be written down.

O is for organisational support. Even valuable, rare and inimitable capabilities may fail to pay off if the structure and systems do not support them. A unique patent is no use without a sales force. The Groupon illustration ties it together: customers valued the daily deals, but thousands of copycats appeared and investors doubted its capabilities were rare or inimitable.

The book then links answers to outcomes. Not valuable means competitive disadvantage. Valuable but not rare means parity. Valuable and rare but imitable means temporary advantage. All four means sustained advantage. VRIO can be applied to functions, single resources or value-chain stages, and if it is hard to tell, split the item into parts. Manufacturing overall may not be an advantage, but product design within it may be.

Now further tools. The value chain describes the activities through which an organisation creates a product or service. Primary activities are inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. Support activities are procurement, technology development, human resource management and infrastructure. It can describe the organisation, host a VRIO analysis of each activity, and show which activities add the most value and where the costs sit. Beyond the firm is the value system, the wider set of links to suppliers, channels and customers. It raises make-or-buy questions and introduces profit pools, the different levels of profit at different points in a system. In computing, microprocessors and software have historically been more profitable than hardware. But spotting a profit pool is not the same as being able to capture it: engineering firms have struggled to move into consulting because neither staff nor clients saw them as consultants.

Activity systems go finer. You map the higher-order strategic themes behind an organisation's success and the clusters of activities supporting each. The lessons are about fit. Activities should reinforce each other, so piecemeal change can damage the system. Combinations may be hard to copy even when single pieces are easy, which links back to inimitability. And you should ask which activities are superfluous: Ryanair dropped many that other airlines take for granted, such as assigned seats and free drinks.

Benchmarking compares performance with others. Industry benchmarking compares you with peers, but the whole industry may be doing badly. Best-in-class benchmarking compares you with the best from any industry, like Southwest Airlines studying Formula One pit stops to speed up refuelling. Its limits are that it can stay at surface level, comparing outputs without explaining why, and that at best it delivers parity, not advantage.

Then SWOT, where this week's two halves meet. Strengths and weaknesses come from this chapter, and opportunities and threats from the environmental analysis before it. SWOT works best when comparative, judged against competitors. The book warns of two dangers. Listing: long lists with no priorities. Its rules are to focus on strengths and weaknesses that differ from rivals, on opportunities and threats relevant to this organisation, and to draw concrete conclusions. And treating SWOT as a substitute for proper analysis, when it is only a summary. To turn it into action, use the TOWS matrix, which pairs internal and external factors to generate options: strengths with opportunities, weaknesses with opportunities, strengths with threats, and weaknesses with threats.

Finally, dynamic capabilities. Advantages can be imitated, become common practice or become redundant as the environment shifts. David Teece's dynamic capabilities are an organisation's ability to renew and recreate its resources and capabilities for changing environments, in contrast to ordinary capabilities that earn a living today. New product development, strategic analysis, alliances and acquisitions are examples. Teece describes three types: sensing opportunities and threats, seizing them with new products or processes, and reconfiguring the organisation's capabilities and investments. Microsoft's move into tablets and cloud services is the illustration. The book links these to its main framework: sensing to strategic position, seizing to strategic choice, and reconfiguring to strategy in action.

It closes with ways of developing capabilities. Internally: building and recombining them, leveraging them across units, and stretching them into new products. Externally: acquisitions, alliances and joint ventures. And ceasing activities that are not central to delivering value.

## Wrapping up

To pull it together: PESTEL and key drivers scan the wide environment. The Five Forces narrow this to the industry and judge how attractive it is. Resources and capabilities turn inward and ask what the organisation has that is valuable, rare, hard to imitate and properly supported. SWOT joins outside and inside, TOWS turns it into options, and dynamic capabilities remind us that none of this stays still.

Three things to be ready to say in the seminar. PESTEL is only useful once narrowed to key drivers. A Five Forces analysis judges the industry, not one firm, and starts with a careful definition. And resources alone rarely give lasting advantage: hard-to-imitate capabilities, and an organisation set up to use them, are what count.
