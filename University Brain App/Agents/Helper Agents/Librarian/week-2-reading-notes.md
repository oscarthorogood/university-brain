## 1. Macro-Environment Analysis (pp. 36–49)

Topic: PESTEL analysis. Chapter chapter 2 runs pp. 34–61.

### Key argument

Managers can be caught out because the macro-environment looks too large and unpredictable to grasp, so it needs a structured scan. PESTEL gives that structure, with different tools for each factor, and identifying key drivers for change turns a long list into the few factors that matter for a particular industry (pp. 36, 49).

### Summary

- The section presents PESTEL as the main tool for scanning the macro-environment: political, economic, social, technological, ecological and legal factors (p. 36).
- It insists the environment is not only markets. Alongside the market environment (suppliers, customers, competitors) sits a nonmarket one (politicians, regulators, NGOs, media) where organisations compete for reputation and legitimacy instead of revenue (pp. 36–37).
- Each letter comes with its own analytical tools rather than a bare checklist, and the section ends by shrinking the resulting long list to a few key drivers for change (pp. 37–49).

### Key points

- (p. 36) PESTEL is one of several similar frameworks (PEST and STEEPLE are others). Its value is in sorting environmental factors into types so that slow-moving changes are not missed.
- (p. 36–37) Market environment: interactions are mainly economic, and pricing and innovation are the key strategies. Nonmarket environment: interactions are with NGOs, politicians, regulators, campaign groups and the media, and the key strategies are lobbying, public relations, networking and collaboration.
- (p. 37) Nonmarket factors matter most where government or regulators are powerful (defence, healthcare), where consumers are sensitive (food), or in small or state-led countries where political, business and media elites are closely linked.
- (p. 37–39) Politics: judge how politicised an environment is on two axes, the state's direct role (customer, supplier, owner, regulator) and exposure to civil-society pressure. Defence scores high on both; food companies have less direct state involvement but face civil-society pressure (Fig. 2.3).
- (p. 39) Political risk analysis has two dimensions: macro versus micro (whole-country risk versus risk specific to a firm or sector, e.g. Japanese carmakers in China) and internal versus external (events inside a country versus knock-on effects from abroad, e.g. an oil-price fall driven by Saudi Arabia hitting Russia and Venezuela).
- (p. 40–41) Economics: watch exchange rates, interest rates and growth, and above all the economic cycle. Investing heavily after several boom years risks over-capacity in the downturn; being over-cautious after slow years risks under-capacity in the recovery (Fig. 2.4). Discretionary-spend industries and high-fixed-cost industries such as airlines are especially cyclical.
- (p. 41) Social factors shape demand and supply through four aspects: demographics, distribution of wealth, geography and culture.
- (p. 41–43) Social factors also include organisational networks. An organisational field is a community of organisations that interact more with each other than with outsiders. Sociograms map these links, and three concepts read them: network density (more links means more effectiveness), broker positions (linking separate groups brings innovativeness) and central hubs (many connections bring power).
- (p. 43) Small worlds are fields where nearly everyone is one or two steps from everyone else. They protect members but are hard for outsiders, such as foreign firms, to enter without insiders' help.
- (p. 43, 45) Technology: five indicators of innovative activity are R&D budgets, patenting, citation analysis, new-product announcements and media coverage. Technology roadmaps project likely product and technology developments forward (Fig. 2.6, Internet of Things). The graphene example: patent applications rose from under 100 a year in 2006 to about 4,000 a decade later, with China accounting for 58 per cent.
- (p. 45–47) Ecological: three challenges are direct pollution obligations, product stewardship across the value chain and product life cycle, and sustainable development. How much pressure an organisation feels depends on the issue itself (certainty, visibility, emotivity), on its organisational field, and on its internal leadership and systems. Motives to respond are responsibility, legitimacy and competitiveness (Fig. 2.7).
- (p. 47–49) Legal: covers regulation, taxation, competition and governance rules, and deregulation can open opportunities (low-cost airlines). The text stretches the L to include informal rules, the institutional environment or 'rules of the game'.
- (p. 47–49) Varieties of capitalism: liberal market economies (US, UK: competition, hostile takeovers, radical innovation, open to foreign firms), coordinated market economies (Germany, Japan: coordination, bank funding, steady innovation, harder for foreigners) and developmental market economies (Brazil, China, India: strong state role, state-influenced banks, foreign firms often disadvantaged).
- (p. 49) Key drivers for change: rather than drown in detail, identify the environmental factors likely to have the biggest impact on a given industry or sector and address those first. The same factor can matter differently by industry (reduced car use affects supermarkets more than retail banks).

## 2. Industry and Sector Analysis (pp. 64–74)

Topic: Industry analysis and Porter's Five Forces. Chapter chapter 3 runs pp. 62–93.

### Key argument

An industry's profit potential is set by the strength of five competitive forces, so managers should define the industry carefully, assess each force, and then look for positions where the organisation can defend itself, exploit the weak forces or influence the strong ones (pp. 64, 73).

### Summary

- Industries differ in how easily firms in them can earn high profits, and Porter's Five Forces (rivalry, threat of entry, threat of substitutes, buyer power, supplier power) explain that difference by describing an industry's structure (p. 64).
- Before the forces are assessed, the industry has to be defined carefully, because a badly drawn boundary hides important competitors or produces a meaningless analysis (p. 65).
- The framework is not an end in itself: the point is to judge whether an industry is worth competing in and to find positions where the organisation can defend itself, exploit weak forces or reshape strong ones (p. 73).

### Key points

- (p. 64) Where competition is intense, buyers and suppliers are strong and entry or substitution is easy, profits are squeezed; pharmaceuticals is given as historically profitable and airlines as underperforming.
- (p. 65) Defining the industry has three parts. Do not define it too broadly or too narrowly (Stockholm taxi example). Treat stages of the value chain separately (iron ore, steel and car-making are different industries). Consider geography and segment. Porter's rule of thumb: they are distinct industries if they differ on more than one force, or a lot on one.
- (p. 66) The analysis is of the whole industry, not one firm. It usually helps to start with rivalry because that identifies the core players.
- (p. 66–67) Five factors raise rivalry: many or evenly matched competitors, low industry growth, high fixed costs (which push firms to cut prices for volume), high exit barriers, and low differentiation (commodity markets such as petrol).
- (p. 67) Five entry barriers: scale and experience effects (including network effects), access to supply or distribution channels, capital requirements, legislation or government action, and expected retaliation by incumbents.
- (p. 68) Illustration 3.1: retail banking has high barriers (economies of scale, network effects, regulation), but regulators are trying to lower them and fintech start-ups are challenging incumbents.
- (p. 69) Substitutes come from outside the industry and cap prices even when little switching happens (Eurostar versus flights). What matters is the price/performance ratio, not price alone (aluminium versus steel).
- (p. 69–70) Buyer power is higher with concentrated buyers, low switching costs, a credible threat of backward vertical integration, and low buyer profits or little effect of the input on the buyer's quality. Buyers are the direct customers, not always end consumers, so the 'strategic customer' (e.g. supermarkets for P&G, the funder in the public sector) is the one to focus on.
- (p. 70) Supplier power is the mirror image: concentrated suppliers, high switching costs, a credible threat of forward vertical integration, and differentiated products or no substitutes. Concentrate on the most important suppliers, which can include labour and finance.
- (p. 71) Some industries need a sixth factor, complementors, which make your offer more attractive to customers or suppliers (app providers for Apple). Considering them adds cooperation to the competition picture, sometimes called co-opetition.
- (p. 71–72) Network effects arise when each extra customer raises a product's value for the others (eBay, Facebook). They can create high entry barriers and lock buyers in, and can work together with complementors, as in smartphones.
- (p. 73) Uses of the analysis: decide which industries to enter or leave (one significantly adverse force can undermine an industry, and low entry barriers are the wrong reason to enter); manage the forces (e.g. raise entry barriers with advertising, buy rivals, differentiate for buyers); and see which competitors are hit hardest by structural change, where strategic group analysis helps.
- (p. 74) Illustration 3.3 gives six steps: define the industry; identify the actors behind each force; determine the underlying factors and overall strength of each force; assess overall attractiveness; assess recent and expected change; and decide how to position the business.

## 3. Resources and Capabilities Analysis (pp. 94–117)

Topic: Resource-based view, VRIO, value chain, SWOT and dynamic capabilities. Chapter chapter 4 runs pp. 94–127.

### Key argument

Performance differences within an industry are explained mainly by organisations' distinctive resources and, above all, capabilities. These give sustained advantage only if they pass VRIO, so strategists should diagnose them with tools such as VRIO, value chain analysis, activity systems, benchmarking and SWOT, and keep renewing them through dynamic capabilities (pp. 95, 104, 116).

### Summary

- The chapter explains why firms in the same industry perform differently: not because of the environment but because of differences in their own resources and capabilities, which are hard for rivals to copy. This is the resource-based view (pp. 94–95).
- Its central test is VRIO: a resource or capability supports sustained advantage only if it is valuable, rare, hard to imitate and backed by the organisation (pp. 98–104).
- The rest of the chapter gives diagnostic tools (VRIO, value chain and system, activity systems, benchmarking, SWOT/TOWS) and closes with dynamic capabilities: the ability to keep renewing capabilities as the environment changes (pp. 104–117).

### Key points

- (p. 95) Organisations are heterogeneous and one firm can find it difficult to obtain or imitate another's resources and capabilities. The resource-based view (Barney) holds that advantage and superior performance come from the distinctiveness of these.
- (p. 96) Resources are the assets an organisation has (nouns) and capabilities are how those assets are deployed (verbs). Deployment matters at least as much as ownership (Table 4.1: e.g. physical, financial and human resources with matching capabilities).
- (p. 96, 98) Threshold resources and capabilities are the 'qualifiers' needed to compete at all, and the bar rises over time (retailers demanding more IT and logistics from suppliers). Distinctive ones are the 'winners' that create advantage because customers value them and competitors struggle to imitate them.
- (p. 98) Hamel and Prahalad's core competences are bundles of linked skills, technologies and activities, which is why they tend to stay unique.
- (p. 99) Value: resources are valuable if customers value what they produce, if they let the firm exploit opportunities or neutralise threats, and if the cost of building them still leaves acceptable returns. Being different is not enough (IKEA's cost-conscious culture is the example).
- (p. 100) Rarity: valuable but common resources give only parity, because rivals can respond quickly (car accessories). Rare ones, such as patents, unique collections, brands or prime locations, give longer-lasting advantage, though rarity should not be assumed to last.
- (p. 100–102) Inimitability has three sources. Complexity: internal linkages between activities and external interconnectedness with customers and partners. Causal ambiguity: competitors cannot tell what causes the advantage (characteristic ambiguity) or which activities depend on which (linkage ambiguity). Culture and history: taken-for-granted routines and path dependency, plus tacit knowledge that is hard to codify.
- (p. 100) Tangible resources can usually be bought or copied over time; advantage more often lies in capabilities, meaning how resources are deployed.
- (p. 102) Organisational support: structure and management systems must let the firm exploit valuable, rare and inimitable capabilities, and complementary capabilities such as a suitable sales force may be needed to realise them (Illustration 4.2, Groupon, p. 103).
- (p. 104) Table 4.2: not valuable gives competitive disadvantage; valuable but not rare gives parity; valuable and rare but imitable gives temporary advantage; valuable, rare, inimitable and supported gives sustained advantage. VRIO can be applied to functions, single resources or value-chain stages.
- (p. 105–106) Value chain: primary activities are inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service; support activities are procurement, technology development, human resource management and infrastructure. It can be used as a generic description, with VRIO on each activity, and to analyse which activities add value and what they cost.
- (p. 106–109) Value system: the wider set of inter-organisational links. It prompts make-or-buy decisions, analysis of cost and price structures across the system, and the location of profit pools (the parts of the system where profit is highest), though spotting a profit pool is not the same as having the capabilities to capture it. Partnering choices also matter.
- (p. 109–111) Activity systems: map the higher-order strategic themes and the clusters of activities behind them to see how activities fit and reinforce each other (Fig. 4.6). Fit is a possible source of sustainable advantage, piecemeal change can damage it, and activities that serve no theme should be questioned (Ryanair dropping conventional airline extras).
- (p. 111–112) Benchmarking compares performance against sector peers or against best-in-class organisations from any industry (Southwest Airlines and Formula One pit stops). Limits: it can stay at surface comparison of outputs, and at best delivers parity, not advantage.
- (p. 112–113) SWOT summarises strengths and weaknesses (this chapter) against opportunities and threats (chapters 2–3) and is most useful when comparative. Dangers: long unprioritised lists, and treating it as a substitute for the underlying analysis. Three rules: focus on relative strengths and weaknesses, on relevant opportunities and threats, and on drawing concrete conclusions.
- (p. 113, 116) The TOWS matrix (Fig. 4.7) turns SWOT into options by pairing internal and external factors: SO, WO, ST and WT strategic options.
- (p. 116–117) Dynamic capabilities (Teece): the ability to renew resources and capabilities as the environment changes, in contrast to 'ordinary' capabilities that support today's business. Three types: sensing, seizing and reconfiguring (Microsoft and tablets and cloud). They link to the book's framework: sensing to position, seizing to choice, reconfiguring to action.
- (p. 117) Ways to develop capabilities: internal (building and recombining, leveraging across units, stretching into new products), external (acquisitions, alliances and joint ventures), and ceasing activities that do not deliver customer value.

## How the three fit together

Macro-environment analysis (PESTEL) and industry analysis (Five Forces) cover the external side of the strategic position and give the opportunities and threats. Resources and capabilities analysis covers the internal side, and SWOT (Resources and Capabilities pp. 112–113) is where the two meet.
