So, three readings. Macro-Environment Analysis, pages thirty-six to forty-nine. Industry and Sector Analysis, pages sixty-four to seventy-four. And Resources and Capabilities Analysis, pages ninety-four to a hundred and seventeen.

Here's the big picture before we dive in, because it makes everything easier to hold onto. All three readings answer one question: how should a manager understand an organisation's strategic position? And the book's answer has two halves. First, look outward. That's the wide macro-environment, using a framework called PESTEL, and then the narrower industry, using Porter's Five Forces. Second, look inward, at the organisation's own resources and capabilities, and ask which of them can give it a real edge over rivals. Then there's a tool called SWOT that pulls the outside and the inside together. Outside, industry, inside, then join them up. Keep that shape in your head and let's go.

Part one. The macro-environment.

This is chapter two of the book, and the part you've been set covers PESTEL analysis, pages thirty-six to forty-nine.

The chapter starts with a worry, and it's a good one. The macro-environment can look far too big, complex and unpredictable to get a grip on. So what happens? Changes creep up on managers until it's too late to avoid the threats or take advantage of the opportunities. Think of the traditional retailers, banks and newspapers that were slow to see what the internet would do to them, or the oil and steel producers who underestimated the effect of China's growth slowing down. The chapter's tools are there to keep organisations alert. It also tells you it has three tools in total: PESTEL, forecasting and scenario analysis. Your set pages are the PESTEL part, so that's where we'll stay.

PESTEL stands for political, economic, social, technological, ecological and legal. It's one of several similar frameworks. You may have met PEST, or STEEPLE. The value of all of them is simply that they sort environmental factors into types, so slow-moving changes don't get missed. That's page thirty-six.

Now here's a point that's easy to skip past, and I'd underline it. The environment isn't only markets. The book splits it into the market environment and the nonmarket environment. The market environment is suppliers, customers and competitors. The interactions are mainly economic. Firms compete for resources, revenue and profit, and the key weapons are pricing and innovation. The nonmarket environment is different. It's NGOs, politicians, government departments, regulators, campaign groups and the media. There, firms aren't competing for sales. They're building reputation, connections, influence and legitimacy, and the key strategies are lobbying, public relations, networking and collaboration. That's pages thirty-six and thirty-seven.

And when does the nonmarket side matter most? The book says three situations. Where government or regulators are powerful, like defence and healthcare. Where consumers are sensitive, like food. And in small or state-led countries where political, business and media elites are closely intertwined. Page thirty-seven.

Okay, the six letters in turn.

Politics first, pages thirty-seven to thirty-nine. The book gives you a neat two-by-two for judging how politicised an environment is. One axis is the state's direct role, whether it's a customer, supplier, owner or regulator. The other is exposure to civil society, meaning lobbyists, campaigners, social media and traditional media. Defence scores high on both, because the armed forces are customers and often owners, and campaigners target the arms trade. Food companies have less direct state involvement, but they're exposed to fair trade campaigners, labour rights groups and health lobbyists. Canals are often state-owned but face little civil-society pressure. And industries can move around that grid quickly. Revelations about internet monitoring, for example, put Amazon and Facebook under much more scrutiny from governments and civil liberties groups.

That connects to the first illustration, a PESTEL of the FANGs, meaning Facebook, Amazon, Netflix and Google. In twenty eighteen the FANG stocks fell by more than ten per cent, and the PESTEL helps explain why. Threats outweighed opportunities on nearly every dimension. Politically, there was growing hostility. Economically, saturation in developed markets, with Netflix missing subscriber targets. Socially, a growing digital detox trend. Technologically, substitutes like encrypted messaging apps. Ecologically, the huge energy use of cloud computing. And legally, the threat of regulation curbing their market power. A nice thing about that example is it shows how the factors interrelate.

Back to politics. Organisations in politicised environments need political risk analysis, which is analysing threats and opportunities from potential political change. The book says it has two dimensions. Macro versus micro: macro risk is about a whole country, like Nigeria or Russia, while micro risk is specific to a particular firm or sector within that country. China might be medium risk overall, but Japanese car companies operating there face extra danger from nationalist boycotts. And internal versus external: internal risk comes from inside the country, like a change of government, which is relatively easy to monitor. External risk is the knock-on effect of events abroad. A fall in oil prices driven by Saudi Arabian politics can hurt Russia and Venezuela, while helping energy importers like India and Japan. That's page thirty-nine.

Economics, pages forty and forty-one. Yes, watch exchange rates, interest rates and growth rates. But the key concept is the economic cycle. Growth tends to rise and fall in cycles, and the underlying message is that good times don't last forever, while bad times eventually lead to recovery. And this matters for timing. Say a company has had several years of rapid growth and is tempted to invest in major new capacity. If it does that at the peak, it may get stuck with expensive over-capacity in the slowdown. On the other hand, after a couple of slow years, a company may become over-cautious, and then face under-capacity when demand recovers, while rivals who invested grab the advantage. So before any strategic investment, the book says, ask where you are in the current economic cycle. Two types of industry are especially vulnerable. Discretionary-spend industries, like furniture, restaurants and cars, where customers can easily put off buying. And high-fixed-cost industries, like airlines, hotels and steel, where firms cut prices in a downturn to keep capacity full, rivals do the same, and you get a price war that leaves everyone with low profits.

Social factors, pages forty-one to forty-three. They matter in two ways. First, they shape demand and supply through four things. Demographics, like ageing populations creating demand for elderly care while the supply of young care workers shrinks. Distribution, meaning how wealth is spread, which changes the size of different markets. Geography, since industries and growth cluster in places, like Silicon Valley or London. And culture, such as the rise of digital natives who expect different things from media, consumption and education.

The second way is more unusual, and it's about networks. The book introduces the organisational field, which is a community of organisations that interact more with each other than with outsiders. It includes competitors, customers and suppliers, but also regulators, trade unions, professions, campaign groups and even individual politicians. You can map these connections with a sociogram, and there are three ideas for reading one. Network density: the more interconnections, the better ideas are shared and the easier it is to mobilise the whole network. Broker positions: an organisation that connects two otherwise separate groups tends to be innovative, because it can combine valuable information from both before anybody else. And central hubs: an organisation connected to many others holds power, because the rest rely on it. The book even shows how a sociogram suggests action. One organisation could undermine another's broker position by connecting directly to the group it brokers.

Then there are small worlds, on page forty-three. That's a network where nearly everyone is just one or two steps from everyone else. Small worlds give members protection and effectiveness, but outsiders, like foreign firms, struggle to break in without insider help. The second illustration is a good example: Israel's Unit 8200, an elite military intelligence unit whose alumni have launched more tech start-ups per head than Stanford. Recruitment favours a buddy system, there's an alumni association of more than fifteen thousand members, and the result is a tight network feeding Israel's cybersecurity industry. A question to ask yourself is who in that network is the hub, and who's the broker.

Technology, pages forty-three to forty-five. The aim is to spot where innovation is happening. There are five indicators. Research and development budgets. Patenting activity. Citation analysis, meaning how widely patents and papers are cited. New product announcements. And media coverage. There are also technology roadmaps, which project future demands, identify technologies that could meet them, and set a timeline. The book's example is the Internet of Things. And the graphene example is striking. Patent applications for graphene went from under a hundred a year in two thousand and six to about four thousand a decade later, and China accounts for fifty-eight per cent of them. So if you want a strong position in graphene, you'll plainly need links with China.

Ecological, pages forty-five to forty-seven. That's the green stuff: pollution, waste and climate change. Regulations can add costs, but they can also create opportunities, like the businesses that grew around mobile phone recycling. The book names three challenges. Direct pollution obligations, which increasingly means minimising pollution in the first place rather than cleaning up at the end of the pipe. Product stewardship, meaning managing the ecological impact across your whole value chain and the whole life of your products, including what happens when customers finish with them. Car makers, for example, are increasingly responsible for recycling old cars. And sustainable development, which asks whether a product can be produced indefinitely into the future.

How much pressure does an organisation actually feel? The book says it depends on three things. First, the ecological issue itself. Issues weigh more when they're certain, visible and emotive. Aircraft pollution gets more attention than shipping pollution because planes are more visible, and polar bears get more attention than hyenas. Second, the organisational field, because active regulators and campaigners raise the pressure, and in a densely connected network it's hard to hide bad behaviour. And third, the organisation itself, meaning its leaders' values and the systems for monitoring behaviour. And why do organisations respond? Three motives. A sense of responsibility. Legitimacy, through regulatory compliance and reputation. And competitiveness, because cutting waste cuts cost, and green products can command a price premium.

Legal, pages forty-seven to forty-nine. Legal factors cover labour, environmental and consumer regulation, taxation, reporting requirements, and rules on ownership, competition and governance. Deregulation can create opportunities, as it did for low-cost airlines. But regulation can also handicap firms, and the illustration here is Juul, the e-cigarette company. Juul entered Israel and was banned within two months because of its nicotine strength. It launched in the UK with a weaker formulation. And it launched in Canada, where the legal regime was more relaxed. Same product, different legal environments, very different strategies.

Here's where the book stretches the idea. The L can cover informal rules as well as formal ones. Together they make up what the book calls the institutional environment, the formal and informal rules of the game. Even regardless of the law, there are explicit norms about respecting the ecological environment, and ignoring them risks outrage among consumers or employees. And these rules differ so much between countries that you get what are called varieties of capitalism. The book describes three.

Liberal market economies, like the United States and United Kingdom. Rules favour competition between companies, aggressive takeovers and free bargaining between management and labour. Companies raise funds from financial markets. These economies support radical innovation and are receptive to foreign firms.

Coordinated market economies, like Germany and Japan. More coordination between companies, often through industry associations. Constraints on hostile takeovers. Companies rely on banks for funding, and family ownership is common. They support steady innovation over the long run, and they're less easy for foreign firms to penetrate.

And developmental market economies, like Brazil, China and India. A strong state role, with the state owning or heavily influencing important companies. Highly regulated labour relations, often state-owned banks, a favouring of long-term capital-intensive projects, and foreign firms often at a disadvantage.

So a macro-environmental analysis of any country should include an assessment of its local variety of capitalism.

Finally, page forty-nine, and this is the bit I'd really remember. Run a PESTEL properly and you get a long, complicated list of issues. The book's advice is not to drown in detail, but to step back and identify the key drivers for change. Those are the environmental factors likely to have the greatest impact on an industry or sector, and on the success or failure of strategies within it. It's how you translate macro factors down to the industry level. The same factor can matter differently for different industries. Social and legislative changes discouraging car use might hit supermarkets harder than retail banks. And once you know your key drivers, you can decide what to do. A supermarket chain facing reduced car use might cut its out-of-town stores and invest in smaller urban and suburban sites.

So if someone asks you the point of the macro-environment reading, here's the one-sentence version. Managers get caught out because the environment feels too big to grasp, so PESTEL gives structure, with different tools for each factor, and identifying key drivers for change turns a long list into the few factors that actually matter.

Part two. Industry analysis and the Five Forces.

Chapter three, pages sixty-four to seventy-four. We're narrowing down now, from the whole environment to the industry.

It starts with a simple observation. Industries differ in how easy it is for firms in them to earn high profits. Pharmaceuticals has historically done very well. Airlines have underperformed. Why? Porter's Five Forces framework says it comes down to five competitive forces: the extent of rivalry between competitors, the threat of entry, the threat of substitutes, the power of buyers, and the power of suppliers. Together they determine the industry's structure and how attractive it is. Where those forces are strong, industries are not attractive to compete in, because rivalry, powerful buyers and suppliers, and the threat of new entrants or substitutes combine to squeeze profits. That's page sixty-four and the diagram in Figure three point two.

But before you analyse any force, you have to define the industry. It sounds boring, but the book gives it real attention, because if you draw the boundary badly, you overlook important things or end up with a meaningless analysis. There are three issues, page sixty-five.

One, don't define it too broadly or too narrowly. Say you're starting a taxi business in Stockholm. The Swedish personal transportation industry is far too broad. Central Stockholm minicabs is too narrow, because taxi firms from the suburbs are competitors too.

Two, think about the value chain. The iron ore industry supplies the steel industry, which supplies car makers and construction. Those are three stages, and they should be analysed separately.

Three, think about geography and segment. The airline industry has different geographical markets, like Europe and China, and different segments, like leisure, business and freight, and the forces are different in each. Porter's rule of thumb is that you're dealing with distinct industries if they differ on more than one competitive force, or differ a lot on any one force.

And bigger firms often analyse several separately. Electrolux, for example, organises major appliances by region but has global segments for small appliances and professional products. When there's no ideal boundary, the crucial thing is to draw it clearly and consistently.

Two more general points, page sixty-six. The analysis is of the whole industry, not one firm or group of firms. And it usually makes sense to start with rivalry, because it defines the core players.

Rivalry, pages sixty-six and sixty-seven. This is the competition between existing players, the incumbents, who aim at the same customers with similar products. Air France and British Airways are rivals. High-speed trains are a substitute. The more rivalry, the worse for incumbents, and the book gives five things that drive it.

One, competitor concentration and balance. Lots of competitors, or ones of roughly equal power, tends to mean intense rivalry, with aggressive price cuts. Less rivalrous industries have one or two dominant players, and the smaller ones avoid challenging them.

Two, industry growth rate. In a growing market, you can grow with it. In a slow or declining one, any growth comes at a rival's expense and meets fierce resistance.

Three, high fixed costs. Firms try to spread the costs by increasing volume, so they cut prices, rivals follow, and everyone suffers in a price war. And where capacity can only be added in big lumps, like a chemical plant or a glass factory, adding capacity creates short-term over-capacity and more competition.

Four, high exit barriers. If closing down is costly, because of redundancy costs or specialised assets nobody would buy, then excess capacity hangs around and firms fight to hold market share.

And five, low differentiation. In a commodity market, like petrol, there's little to stop customers switching, so the only way to compete is on price.

Second force, the threat of entry, page sixty-seven. The greater the threat of entry, the worse for incumbents. Attractive industries have high barriers to entry, which are the factors a new entrant has to overcome. The book gives five.

Scale and experience. Economies of scale, like pharmaceutical firms spreading huge research and marketing costs over big volumes, and experience curve effects, where incumbents have learned to work more efficiently. There are also demand-side economies of scale, known as network effects, and we'll come back to those.

Access to supply or distribution channels. Incumbents may control them, sometimes through vertical integration, sometimes through loyalty. Though some entrants, like Dell and Amazon, got round this by selling direct to consumers.

Capital requirements. Pharmaceuticals needs huge research investment over many years.

Legislation or government action, from patent protection to tariffs. And of course deregulation can strip that protection away, as it did in airlines.

And expected retaliation. If a would-be entrant believes an incumbent will fight back hard, with a price war or a marketing blitz, that alone can be enough to deter them.

The illustration on page sixty-eight is retail banking. The barriers have traditionally been very high: economies of scale, network effects and regulation. In the UK, the Big Five dominate. Incumbent banks often lobby for regulation because, once they've adapted to it, it raises barriers against newcomers. But regulators face a dilemma. They want stability, which means strict rules, and they also want competition and efficiency, so they've started encouraging entrants, for instance with a start-up unit to help new banks. And on top of that, over a hundred financial technology start-ups are using new technology to challenge incumbents, who are held back by regulation and legacy systems. So the barriers might be busted.

Third force, substitutes, page sixty-nine. A substitute is a product or service that does a similar job but has a different nature. Aluminium for steel, a tablet for a laptop, charities for public services. Managers tend to focus on direct competitors and neglect substitutes. But they can shrink demand, even make a product obsolete. And there doesn't have to be much actual switching for the threat to bite, because the risk of substitution puts a cap on what you can charge. Eurostar has no direct competitor between Paris and London, but the cost of flights ultimately limits its prices. Two points to remember. Substitutes come from outside the industry, which is why the concept is valuable, because it forces managers to look further afield. And it's the price-to-performance ratio that matters, not just price. Aluminium costs more than steel, but it's lighter and resists corrosion, so in some car applications it wins.

Fourth force, buyer power, pages sixty-nine and seventy. Buyers are the organisation's immediate customers, not necessarily the ultimate consumers. Powerful buyers can demand low prices or costly improvements. The book gives four conditions. Concentrated buyers: a few large customers account for most sales, like the big retailers in European grocery. Low switching costs: buyers can easily move between suppliers, typical for standardised products like steel, or when buyers are fully informed. Buyer competition threat: the buyer can supply itself, which is called backward vertical integration, moving back to sources of supply. Some steel companies gained power over iron ore suppliers by buying their own ore sources. And low buyer profits and impact on quality: for organisational buyers, if their own profits are squeezed, or the input barely affects the quality of what they sell, they get very price sensitive.

The distinction between buyers and consumers really matters. For Procter and Gamble or Unilever, the buyers are retailers like Carrefour or Tesco, not ordinary shoppers, and those retailers have far more negotiating power. So the book introduces the strategic customer, the powerful buyer strategy should be mainly aimed at. In the public sector, that's typically the provider of funds, not the user. For a pharmaceutical company, the strategic customer is the hospital, not the patient.

Fifth force, supplier power, page seventy. Suppliers provide what the organisation needs, including fuel, raw materials, equipment, labour and finance. The factors are the mirror image of buyer power. Concentrated suppliers: iron ore is in the hands of three main producers, leaving fragmented steel companies in a weak position. High switching costs: Microsoft is powerful because moving from one operating system to another is a hassle. Supplier competition threat: suppliers can enter the industry themselves or cut out intermediaries. Airlines got tough with travel agents once online booking gave them a direct route to customers. That's forward vertical integration, moving closer to the ultimate customer. And differentiated products: suppliers with strong brands, like Procter and Gamble with Gillette, keep negotiating power, as do groups with no substitutes, like pilots' unions. Most organisations have many suppliers, so concentrate on the most important types. Star footballers are a great example. They've pushed their rewards sky-high, while even leading clubs, their buyers, struggle to make money.

Now the extra bit, page seventy-one. Some industries need a sixth factor: complementors. A complementor is an organisation that makes your business more attractive to customers or suppliers. App providers are complementors to Apple, because customers value an iPhone more when there are lots of appealing apps. On the supply side, competing airlines can be complementary for Boeing, because it's more attractive to invest in improvements for two customers than one. The key shift is in perspective. Porter's forces have organisations battling for a share of industry value, while complementors may cooperate to increase the total value. So you consider both cooperation and competition, sometimes called co-opetition.

Related is network effects, or network externalities. That's when one customer of a product has a positive effect on its value for other customers. The more people on eBay, the better it is for everyone on eBay. When network effects are present, they can make an industry structurally attractive, with high barriers, low rivalry and locked-in buyers, because rivals can't compete with a bigger network. The number of users is often called the installed base. And sometimes complementors and network effects work together. Apple's apps attract users, and more users mean more network effects, which attract more users. The illustration on page seventy-two, Facebook's network fears, shows both sides. Facebook controls the biggest social networks, and it's built high switching costs, but internet history is littered with social networks that rose and collapsed, like Friendster and MySpace. Facebook responded by buying Instagram and WhatsApp, and it saw off Google's attempts, like Google Plus.

Okay, so what do you do with all of this? Page seventy-three. The aim isn't to list the strength of each force. It's to decide whether the industry is a good one to compete in, and whether there are advantageous positions where the organisation can defend itself, exploit weak forces or influence the forces in its favour. That leads to three questions.

One, which industries should we enter or leave? Attractive industries are those where the forces are weak. Managers should invest where the forces work in their favour and avoid, or disinvest from, markets where they're strongly unfavourable. Two warnings. Entrepreneurs sometimes pick markets because entry barriers are low, and unless barriers are likely to rise quickly, that's precisely the wrong reason. And just one significantly adverse force can be enough to undermine an industry, for example powerful buyers forcing down prices and extracting all the potential profit.

Two, how can the forces be managed? Industry structures aren't fixed. Managers can influence them. Generally, exploit and influence weak forces, and neutralise strong ones. If entry barriers are low, raise them, for example by spending on advertising to build loyalty. Buy up competitors to reduce rivalry or increase power over suppliers or buyers. If buyers are strong, differentiate for a specific customer group to increase loyalty and switching costs. And one approach to finding differentiated positions is what's called Blue Ocean thinking, which the book covers later.

Three, how are competitors affected differently? Not every competitor feels structural change equally. If barriers rise because of more research or advertising spending, smaller players might be squeezed out. Growing buyer power hurts small competitors most. Strategic group analysis helps here, which is also covered later.

And the book adds that five forces analysis isn't only for private companies. In the public and charity sectors, you might switch focus from a crowded area to a less rivalrous one where you can be more distinctive, or reduce dependence on powerful, expensive suppliers like energy or scarce skills.

Finally, page seventy-four, a six-step procedure, worked through with an example of Emily, who wants to start a coffee shop.

Step one, define the industry clearly. Which stages of the value chain, which products or services and segments, and what geographic scope. Emily's competitors include cafés, chains, fast food outlets, kiosks and restaurants, and it depends whether she's urban or rural.

Step two, identify the actors behind each force. Competitors, buyers and buyer groups, suppliers and supplier groups, potential entrants and substitutes. Emily's suppliers aren't just coffee, but also the landlord and labour.

Step three, determine the underlying factors and total strength of each force. Which are strong, which are weak, and why.

Step four, assess the overall industry structure and attractiveness. How attractive is it, which forces control profitability, and are more profitable competitors better placed against the forces.

Step five, assess recent and expected changes. What are the possible positive and negative changes, and are new entrants or competitors changing the structure. Emily has to think about the proliferation of coffee chains, and pubs and bakeries improving their coffee.

And step six, decide how to position the business. Can you exploit weak forces, neutralise strong ones, exploit industry change, or influence the structure in your favour? Emily might find a concept that attracts a certain customer group even in a crowded urban market, which could neutralise some competition and entry threats.

So the one-sentence version of this reading. An industry's profit potential is set by the strength of five forces, so define the industry carefully, assess each force, and look for positions where the organisation can defend itself, exploit the weak forces or influence the strong ones.

Part three. Resources and capabilities.

Chapter four, pages ninety-four to a hundred and seventeen. And now we turn inward.

It starts with a puzzle. Companies in the same industry, in the same environment, perform very differently. BMW has been consistently successful based on its engineering and brand. Chrysler has found it harder, and Saab went out of business. The book's explanation is that it's not so much the characteristics of the environment, but differences in organisation-specific resources and capabilities. Two ideas underlie this. Organisations are heterogeneous, meaning they genuinely differ in their resources and capabilities. And it can be difficult for one organisation to obtain or imitate another's. Put those together and you get the resource-based view of strategy, associated with Jay Barney: competitive advantage and superior performance are explained by the distinctiveness of an organisation's resources and capabilities. That's pages ninety-four and ninety-five.

Definitions, page ninety-six. Resources are the assets an organisation has or can call on. Capabilities are the ways those assets are deployed. A handy shorthand: resources are what we have, nouns. Capabilities are what we do, verbs. So machines, buildings, patents and databases are physical resources, with capabilities like efficiency, flexibility and utilisation. Cash flow and balance sheets are financial resources, with the capability to raise funds and manage cash. Managers and employees are human resources, with capabilities like gaining experience, building relationships and innovating. And the book stresses that deployment matters at least as much as ownership. There's no point having state-of-the-art equipment if it isn't used effectively. Some writers use other words, like competences or intangible assets, but the book uses resources and capabilities. Illustration four point one, on page ninety-seven, shows executives from the Australian Red Cross, AstraZeneca and Infosys all emphasising capabilities in their strategy.

Next, a distinction between threshold and distinctive resources and capabilities, pages ninety-six to ninety-eight. Threshold ones are the qualifiers. They're what you need just to compete at all and reach parity with competitors. Without them, you couldn't survive. And the bar keeps moving. Suppliers to big retailers now need far more sophisticated IT and logistics than they did a decade ago, just to stay in business. But threshold resources don't create advantage. Distinctive ones are the winners. They're valued by customers and hard for competitors to imitate. That might be a distinctive resource, like a long-established brand, or a distinctive capability, a way of doing things unique to the organisation. Apple, for instance, has distinctive smartphone technologies and a powerful brand, plus distinctive capabilities in design and understanding consumers. The book also mentions core competences, from Hamel and Prahalad. Distinctive capabilities tend to stay unique because they're a bundle of linked skills and technologies, not a single skill.

Now, the heart of the chapter, pages ninety-eight to a hundred and three. The VRIO test. Four criteria for whether resources and capabilities can give sustained competitive advantage. Value, rarity, inimitability and organisational support.

V is for value, page ninety-nine. Resources and capabilities are valuable when they create something customers value and let the organisation respond to opportunities or threats. Three components. Value to customers. It sounds obvious, but managers often build on things they see as valuable that don't meet customers' critical success factors, or treat something as valuable just because it's unique. Being different isn't a basis for advantage in itself. Second, taking advantage of opportunities and neutralising threats. IKEA's cost-conscious culture and size lower its costs, which addresses an opportunity for low-priced designed furniture. And third, cost. If the cost of developing the resource eats up the returns, it's not worth it.

R is for rarity, page a hundred. Valuable but common resources are unlikely to give advantage, because rivals can respond quickly. Car makers adding accessories is the example. As soon as customers value a gadget, everyone introduces it. Rare resources, possessed by one organisation or a few, give more lasting advantage. Patents, unique collections, powerful brands, prime retail locations, special relationships with customers or suppliers. But it's dangerous to assume rarity will last.

I is for inimitability, pages a hundred to a hundred and two, and it gets the most space. Are the resources and capabilities difficult and costly for competitors to copy, obtain or substitute? Here's a key point. It's unusual for advantage to come from differences in tangible resources, because over time they can usually be bought or imitated. An IT system can be bought on the open market. Advantage more often lies in capabilities, in how resources are deployed and managed, because those involve intangible barriers and linkages between activities, skills, knowledge and people. There are three sources.

Complexity. Internal linkages, where tightly knit activities have built up over years. Even with a map of them, a competitor couldn't replicate the numerous interactions. That's why IKEA and Ryanair still enjoy advantages despite countless case studies about them. And external interconnectedness, where activities are developed with customers or partners so they become dependent. Apple's links with app developers and music labels are the example.

Causal ambiguity. Competitors can't work out what's causing the advantage. Characteristic ambiguity is when the characteristic itself is hard to understand, perhaps because it's rooted in culture or tacit knowledge, like a fashion buyer's feel for spotting trends. Linkage ambiguity is when they can't tell which activities depend on which. Sometimes even the managers admit they don't fully understand all the linkages.

And culture and history. Capabilities embedded in culture, where coordination happens naturally because everyone knows their part, as in high-performing sports teams or hospital operating theatres. And path dependency, meaning the particular history through which capabilities developed, which is specific to the organisation and can't be copied. Related is the distinction between tacit knowledge, which is personal, context-specific and hard to communicate, and explicit knowledge, which can be written down. Companies trying to share knowledge through IT systems have found that some knowledge can be codified, but the knowledge that truly gives advantage is very hard to.

O is for organisational support, page a hundred and two. Even valuable, rare and inimitable capabilities may fail to pay off if the organisation isn't set up to exploit them, through its structure and its formal and informal management control systems. A unique patent is no use without the right sales force. Supporting capabilities like this are called complementary capabilities. On its own, each may not give advantage, but they help exploit the ones that do. Illustration four point two, on page a hundred and three, is Groupon. Customers valued the daily deals, and it grew incredibly fast. But thousands of copycats appeared, and investors doubted that its capabilities were rare or inimitable. The CEO argued the operational complexity was hard to replicate, but investors weren't convinced, and the shares fell by eighty per cent at their low.

Then the book links VRIO to outcomes, page a hundred and four, and this is a table worth knowing. If a capability isn't valuable, that's a competitive disadvantage. Valuable but not rare, competitive parity. Valuable and rare but easy to imitate, a temporary competitive advantage. Valuable, rare, inimitable and supported by the organisation, sustained competitive advantage. VRIO can be applied to whole functions, to individual resources and capabilities, or to sections of the value chain. And if it's hard to tell whether something gives sustained advantage, split it into parts. Manufacturing as a whole may not give advantage, but product engineering or design within it might.

Now more diagnostic tools, section four point four.

The value chain and value system, pages a hundred and five to a hundred and nine. The value chain describes the categories of activity within an organisation that together create a product or service. Porter's version has primary activities: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. And support activities: procurement, technology development, human resource management, and infrastructure. The book gives three uses. As a generic description, asking which categories best describe the business and which matter most for advantage. As a base for VRIO analysis of individual activities. And to analyse value and cost, which activities add most value, and where costs sit. Organisations often find that central services have grown into a disproportionate cost that doesn't add value.

Beyond the firm is the value system, the wider set of links to suppliers, distribution channels and customers. It raises three sets of questions. Make or buy: which activities must stay in-house because they're central to advantage, and which can be outsourced. Costs and prices across the whole system. And profit pools, meaning the different levels of profit at different parts of the system. In computing, microprocessors and software have historically been more profitable than hardware manufacture. But it's one thing to spot a profit pool and another to capture it. Engineering firms have found it hard to move into consulting, because their staff lacked consultancy capabilities or clients didn't see them that way. Then there's partnering, meaning who the best partners are, and whether they should be suppliers or alliance partners. Illustration four point three, on page a hundred and eight, shows a Nepalese poultry company, the Valley Group, that integrated along its value system to secure steady supply in difficult conditions, by setting up its own chick, feed, buying and cold storage operations.

Activity systems, pages a hundred and nine to a hundred and eleven. This goes finer. You map the higher-order strategic themes behind an organisation's success, then the clusters of activities that support each. The book's example is a Scandinavian communications consultancy, Geelmuyden Kiese, whose core theme is its knowledge of power dynamics in decision making. Around it are themes like working at a strategic level, a clear stance on integrity and openness, freedom with responsibility, values-based recruitment of junior staff, and strong financial incentives. The book draws four lessons. It relates to the value chain, but gives a deeper view of complexity. It stresses linkages and fit: activities should pull in the same direction, so piecemeal change or tinkering can damage the system, and change has to be managed across the whole. It relates to VRIO, because individual components might be easy to copy, but in combination they may be complex, causally ambiguous and rooted in culture and history. And it asks about superfluous activities, activities that don't serve the strategy. Ryanair systematically dropped many that other airlines have, like adjustable seats, assigned seats and in-flight meals.

Benchmarking, pages a hundred and eleven to a hundred and twelve. That's comparing your organisation with others. Two approaches. Industry or sector benchmarking, against similar organisations, though the whole industry may be performing badly and losing out to other industries. And best-in-class benchmarking, against the best performers from any industry, which can challenge managers' mindsets. Southwest Airlines improved refuelling times by studying Formula One pit stops. It has two limits. Surface comparisons: it may show you're worse at customer service without showing why. And parity: at best it gets you to a threshold level, and to get advantage you need your own distinctive capabilities.

SWOT, pages a hundred and twelve to a hundred and sixteen. This is where this week's two halves meet. SWOT summarises the strengths and weaknesses from this chapter's analysis against the opportunities and threats from the environmental analysis in chapters two and three. The aim is to see how far your strengths and weaknesses can deal with the changes in the environment. The Pharmcare illustration, on pages a hundred and fourteen and a hundred and fifteen, scores strengths and weaknesses against environmental changes on a plus five to minus five scale. Its strengths were a flexible sales force, a well-known brand and a healthcare education department. Its weaknesses included weak information technology capabilities, low research productivity and over-reliance on a leading product. And the book stresses that SWOT is most useful when it's comparative, judged against competitors, and the Pharmcare example compares it with rivals.

There are two main dangers. Listing: producing long lists when what matters is priorities. Three rules help. Focus on strengths and weaknesses that differ from competitors, and leave out areas where you're on a par. Focus on opportunities and threats directly relevant to the specific organisation and industry, not general ones. And summarise and draw concrete conclusions. The second danger is that SWOT is a summary, not a substitute. Used alone, managers rely on preconceived, biased views and get vague, general statements.

To turn SWOT into action, use the TOWS matrix, figure four point seven, on page a hundred and thirteen. It pairs internal and external factors to generate four kinds of option. Strengths with opportunities, using strengths to take advantage of opportunities. Weaknesses with opportunities, taking advantage of opportunities by overcoming weaknesses. Strengths with threats, using strengths to avoid threats. And weaknesses with threats, minimising weaknesses and avoiding threats. For Pharmcare, that might be retraining the sales force, developing its information technology systems, and building on its healthcare education department to serve better-informed patients.

Last section, dynamic capabilities, pages a hundred and sixteen and a hundred and seventeen. Everything so far might make advantage sound static. But capabilities that once gave success can be imitated, become common practice, or become redundant as the environment changes. So, David Teece introduced dynamic capabilities: an organisation's ability to renew and recreate its resources and capabilities to meet the needs of changing environments. They contrast with ordinary capabilities, the ones for efficient operations that let you earn a living now by producing and selling a similar product to similar customers. Dynamic capabilities create, extend or modify those ordinary ones. New product development is a typical example. So is strategic analysis, and so is outlet expansion by chains like Starbucks or Zara. They can also be formal systems, like reorganisation, recruitment and management development, or cooperating through alliances and acquisitions. There's a tension between ordinary and dynamic capabilities, sometimes called the exploration and exploitation trade-off, which comes up again later in the book.

Teece suggests three generic types. Sensing: constantly scanning and exploring opportunities, through research and development and investigating customer needs. Microsoft sensed the threats and opportunities in tablets and cloud computing. Seizing: addressing a sensed opportunity with new products, services, processes or activities. Microsoft launched its own tablets and cloud services. And reconfiguring: renewing capabilities and investing in new technologies, manufacturing or markets. Microsoft's move needed major changes to its PC and game console resources, discarding some old capabilities, acquiring and building new ones, and recombining them.

And here's a neat link back to the book's main framework. Sensing relates to understanding strategic position. Seizing relates to strategic choices. And reconfiguring relates to strategy in action. Teece also says dynamic capabilities have micro-foundations in people's behaviour, like how decisions get taken, personal relationships, and entrepreneurial and intuitive skills.

The chapter closes with ways of developing capabilities, page a hundred and seventeen. Internally, there are three. Building and recombining capabilities, which needs entrepreneurship and intrapreneurship skills, and maybe new venture units outside the usual rules. Leveraging capabilities, taking something one unit does well, like customer service in one region, and spreading it, though transfer isn't easy because of the difficulties of managing change. And stretching capabilities, building new products or services out of existing ones, which is the basis of related diversification. Externally, capabilities can be obtained through acquisitions, alliances and joint ventures. And there's ceasing activities, where activities not central to delivering value are dropped, outsourced or made cheaper, so you retain what's central to your advantage and cut costs elsewhere.

So the one-sentence version of this reading. Performance differences within an industry are mainly explained by distinctive resources and, above all, capabilities. They give sustained advantage only if they pass VRIO, so use tools like VRIO, the value chain, activity systems, benchmarking and SWOT to diagnose them, and keep renewing them through dynamic capabilities.

Wrapping up.

So how do the three fit together? PESTEL and key drivers for change scan the wide environment and find the trends that matter. The Five Forces narrow that down to the industry, and tell you how attractive it is and where a defensible position might be. Together those two give you the opportunities and threats, the outside half. Resources and capabilities analysis covers the inside half, asking what the organisation has that's valuable, rare, hard to imitate and properly supported. SWOT is where inside and outside meet, TOWS turns that into options, and dynamic capabilities remind us that none of it stays still.

Three things to be ready to say in the seminar. One, PESTEL is only useful once you've narrowed it to key drivers for change, so avoid long lists. Two, a Five Forces analysis judges the industry, not one firm, and it starts with a careful definition of the industry. And three, resources alone rarely give lasting advantage. Hard-to-imitate capabilities, and an organisation set up to use them, are what count.

That's your week two reading. Good luck in the lecture and the seminar.
