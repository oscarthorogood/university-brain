## 📝 Transcript

**Host A:** This week's two readings are chapters seven and eight of Whittington and colleagues, *Exploring Strategy*, 12th edition, 2020. Chapter seven is business strategy and models. Chapter eight is corporate strategy. Both are due Monday 5 October, ahead of the Tuesday lecture.

**Host B:** Chapter seven first. It separates two questions. Business strategy is how to compete in a marketplace. Corporate strategy, chapter eight, is whether you should be in that business at all (p. 202). Their example is Nestlé's ice cream unit.

**Host A:** The core idea is competitive advantage: creating value for users greater than the cost of supplying them and superior to rivals (p. 204). Two routes: structurally lower costs, or differentiation customers value enough to pay more for. Porter adds scope, broad or narrow.

**Host B:** Three generic strategies. Cost leadership is becoming the systematically lowest-cost organisation in a domain (p. 205). Four cost drivers: input costs, economies of scale, experience, and product or process design (pp. 205–206).

**Host A:** Being second-lowest cost is a disadvantage, and low cost cannot ignore quality: the cost leader needs parity or proximity on valued features (p. 207).

**Host B:** Differentiation is uniqueness valued enough to allow a price premium (p. 209). Drivers: product and service attributes, customer relationships, complements (pp. 209–210). The extra cost must not exceed the extra price (p. 210).

**Host A:** Focus targets a narrow segment and tailors to it (p. 210), as cost focus (Iceland Foods) or differentiation focus (Ecover). It depends on distinct segment needs, distinct value chains and viable economics (p. 212).

**Host B:** Porter warned about being stuck in the middle. Hybrids are possible through organisational separation or technological or managerial innovation, but with caution (pp. 212–213).

**Host A:** The Strategy Clock looks at price rather than cost, with four zones: differentiation, low price, hybrid, and non-competitive (pp. 213–214).

**Host B:** Chapter eight. Diversification is increasing the range of products or markets served (p. 238). Ansoff's matrix gives four directions. Market penetration is more share of current markets with current products; the constraints are retaliation, legal limits and economic conditions (pp. 239–241).

**Host A:** Product and service development is new products for existing markets, costly and risky (p. 241). Market development is existing products into new users or geographies (p. 242). Unrelated diversification goes into completely new markets and products; the extreme form is conglomerate diversification, with the "conglomerate discount" (p. 242).

**Host B:** Performance research finds an inverted U: related or limited diversifiers tend to outperform, but it is an average, and LVMH is a conglomerate that works (p. 246).

**Host A:** Vertical integration is entering activities where the organisation is its own supplier or customer (p. 246). Backward is into inputs, forward is into outputs (p. 247). Dangers: investment that dilutes returns, and different capabilities (p. 247).

**Host B:** Integrate or outsource? Williamson's transaction cost framework: integrate when there are few alternative suppliers, the activity is complex and changing, and there are specific-asset investments (p. 248).

**Host A:** The BCG matrix uses market share and growth (pp. 256–257). Stars, question marks, cash cows, dogs. Four problems: vague definitions, capital-market assumptions, harsh treatment of cows and dogs, and ignoring commercial links (p. 258).

**Host B:** For Tuesday: chapter seven is how one business competes. Chapter eight is which businesses to be in, which direction to grow, and how to manage the portfolio.

## 💡 Takeaways

> [!tip] What to remember
> - Competitive advantage = value above cost of supply and above rivals (p. 204).
> - Generic strategies: cost leadership (p. 205), differentiation (p. 209), focus (p. 210); beware "stuck in the middle" (p. 212).
> - Ansoff directions: market penetration, product development, market development, unrelated diversification (pp. 238–242).
> - Diversification and performance: inverted U (p. 246).
> - Vertical integration (p. 246); integrate versus outsource by transaction costs (p. 248).
> - BCG matrix: stars, question marks, cash cows, dogs (pp. 256–257); limits (p. 258).
> - Page numbers are printed pages; the Business Models section (p. 221) is outside the set pages and left out.
